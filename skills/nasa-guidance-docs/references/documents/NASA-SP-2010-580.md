**Scope, plainly.** Volume 1's own purpose statement: "to present the overall framework for System Safety and to provide the general concepts needed to implement the framework. The treatment addresses activities throughout the system life cycle to assure that the system meets safety performance requirements and is as safe as reasonably practicable" (§1 Purpose, printed p.1 / PDF p.17). It is a handbook: it structures the argument, it does not supply the evidence — Volume 1 says so on its own first page, deferring "the development of the evidence" to Volume 2 (NASA/SP-2014-612).

**The Risk-Informed Safety Case (RISC), its load-bearing content.** §5.2 (printed p.71 / PDF p.87) sets out a hierarchy: a project writes safety **objectives**, rephrases each as a **claim** that the objective has been met, supports each claim with **evidence**, and links evidence to claims with an **argument** using "logically valid rules of inference." Sub-claims sit *inside* evidence, not between argument and evidence — "Each sub-claim is a claim in its own right," and a lower unit's whole case can serve as a sub-claim of the case above it (same page). Evidence splits into two named kinds, verbatim from the Executive Summary (printed p.xiii / PDF p.15): "direct or demonstrative" (results from operational experience, testing, and analysis) and "indirect or validating" (factors showing the first kind is valid and applicable — "the validity of the assumptions made, the relevance of the environments used or assumed, the degree of verification and validation of the models, the qualifications of the personnel, the robustness of the quality assurance processes, the quality of management oversight"). §5.2.2 runs the same direct/indirect split a second time, applied to arguments rather than evidence (printed pp.73–74 / PDF pp.89–90). Arguments themselves are classed as "deterministic, probabilistic, or qualitative" (§5.2, printed p.71 / PDF p.87) — not "qualitative or quantitative," a phrasing that does not occur in this sense anywhere in the 120 pages (verified by full-text search). The whole case is presented to a named decision-maker who "consciously accept[s] or reject[s] each claim" against a checklist deliberately "not shaped like" the case it grades (§5.5, printed p.86 / PDF p.102).

**AI and machine-learning touchpoints.** None found in the text (searched: artificial intelligence, machine learning, autonom*, neural, algorithm, software, automat*). The handbook has no published Rationale section, so there is no NASA-stated per-clause reason to test against AI directly (our reading). Two features of the RISC framework plausibly transfer to a learned component regardless: evidence is explicitly allowed to be an assumption, so an unproven premise is carried visibly rather than hidden; and the handbook prizes two independent arguments for one claim "so that it can tolerate flaws in a single argument" (§5.2, printed p.71 / PDF p.87) (our reading). Where it strains: three of the named indirect-evidence examples ("qualifications of the personnel," "robustness of the quality assurance processes," "quality of management oversight") are about people and process and do not obviously map onto a learned model (our reading); the one that does transfer cleanly, "the degree of verification and validation of the models," routes toward NASA-STD-7009B's model-credibility framework.

**Connections and traps.** Volume 2 (NASA/SP-2014-612, November 2014) is the stated companion that operationalizes this framework into acquirer/provider guidance — cite that document for implementation detail, this one for the framework itself. The handbook's own top-level claims include "Unknown and un-quantified safety hazards are managed" (Figure 2-3, printed p.9 / PDF p.25) — a RISC built on this handbook is only as strong as the hazard identification feeding it, which the handbook itself does not test. The checklist-independence rule (§5.5) means an evaluator built the same way as the case being evaluated is a structural warning sign, not a compliance detail (our reading).
