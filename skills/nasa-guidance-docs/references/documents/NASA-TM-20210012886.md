**Scope, plainly.** A 35-page report that proposes an ethics vocabulary and initial governance recommendations for NASA's use of artificial intelligence (AI), defined in the document's own words as *"any computerized capability to perceive, reason, learn, and act"* (p.7). It distinguishes three capability tiers, the first being Artificial Narrow Intelligence (ANI) — *"operates at less than human ability. As of publication, all AI is ANI... it does not generalize, nor does it understand the larger situation like a human would"* (p.7); the other two tiers (AGI and beyond) follow in the same list. The framework states it drew its structure from external frameworks — the Department of Defense's Defense Innovation Board, Gartner, and ACT-IAC — and *"selected the Gartner framework as our baseline because its elements generalized well, avoided overlap among themselves, and could succinctly map to NASA's needs"* (p.7, our paraphrase of the comparison table discussion). Appendix A reproduces the DoD Defense Innovation Board's ethical principles for comparison; Appendix B gives ethics questions for project leads and principal investigators; Appendix E adds definitions.

**Six ethical AI principles (verbatim, p.7).** The framework's core content is six named principles, each with a short elaboration:
- *"Fair."* — "must include considerations regarding how to treat people, including refining solutions to mitigate discrimination and bias, preventing covert manipulation, and supporting diversity and inclusion."
- *"Explainable and Transparent."* — "Solutions must clearly state if, when, and how an AI system is involved, and AI logic and decisions must be explainable."
- *"Accountable."* — "Organizations and individuals must be accountable for the systems they create, and organizations must implement AI governance structures to provide oversight."
- *"Secure and Safe."* — "AI systems must respect privacy and do no harm. Humans must monitor and guide machine learning processes."
- *"Human-Centric and Societally Beneficial."* — "AI systems must obey human legal systems and must provide benefits to society. At the current state of AI, humans must remain in charge, though future advancements may cause reconsideration of this requirement."
- *"Scientifically and Technically Robust."* — "AI systems must adhere to the scientific method NASA applies to all problems, be informed by scientific theory and data, robustly tested in implementation, well-documented, and peer reviewed in the scientific community."

It recommends, in its own "must" register (recommendation, not requirement), that *"an AI advisory body must be established to participate in existing governance processes and provide a new AI review function as needed"* and that ethical-AI considerations be folded into *"existing review cycles (e.g., software reviews, project reviews, etc.)"* (p.8).

**Named offices/roles and responsibilities (our extraction).** No office or role with assigned, standing responsibilities is established by this document — only the recommendation above that one be created. It predates NASA's Chief AI Officer and AI Strategy Board (NC-1000.61), neither of which the text names (our reading; searched the extracted text for "Chief AI Officer" and "Strategy Board" — zero hits). It does name the "NASA Data Governance Board" and "Carnegie Mellon's Software Engineering Institute" as external groups consulted in developing the framework (p.6, our paraphrase), not as governance roles.

**Connections and traps.**
- 🔴 Do not treat any `shall` in this document as a NASA requirement — its own convention reserves `shall` for a separate "accompanying" policy document, and none is identified in the text (our reading).
- Companion document: `NASA-Responsible-AI-Plan.md` (NASA's EO 13960 response), also reference-and-intent only, not governance.
- Dated: "as of publication, all AI is ANI" is a 2021 factual claim, written before generative AI's mainstreaming and before OMB M-24-10/M-25-21 and NASA's AI Strategy Board existed; do not read it as a standing claim about current AI capability (our reading).
- The six principles above are this framework's own synthesis of outside frameworks (Gartner, DoD, ACT-IAC), not independently NASA-originated (our reading, p.7) — useful as vocabulary, not as a citation that NASA has adopted any one of them as policy.
