## What it is for, plainly

This is NASA's top-level policy statement on safety and mission success — the Agency's stated doctrine for how much risk is acceptable, who sets that level, and what has to be true before anyone can say a mission is on track. It names no engineering method and sets no numbered requirement; it states policy and tells named officials, by title, what they are responsible and accountable for. The requirements that carry out this policy live in the NPR family beneath it (the 8700/8705/8715/8719 series).

## What it obliges (or advises)

NPDs state policy rather than numbered `shall` requirements — this directive's own extracted text contains zero occurrences of "shall." Its duty form is "is responsible and accountable for." What it states:

- Each mission must have a stated risk posture for crew safety and mission success, weighed against the mission's benefits, strategic importance, and the consequences of failure, and that posture then informs how the mission is formulated, built, and assured (§1 a(1), p.1).
- Progress toward safety and mission-success objectives must be **substantiated, monitored, and independently evaluated throughout the lifecycle, based on systematic argumentation, explicit assumptions, and objective evidence** (§1 a(2)(e), p.1) — program and project managers carry this duty by name (§5 c(3), p.4).
- Someone must formally accept the case that crew safety and mission success are within the stated risk posture, or on track to be (§1 a(3), p.1).
- 🔴 **Where no accepted standard exists for a novel or unique hazard, NASA policy is to consult subject matter experts on strategies to reach an acceptable level of risk** (§1 b(4), p.2).
- Safety standards are to be chosen with performance-based approaches prioritized over prescriptive ones (§1 b(3), p.2).
- Technical Authorities concur or non-concur with risk-acceptance decisions, and separately concur or non-concur with the adequacy of the assurance case supporting them (§5 d, p.4).
- A robust safety culture is policy: reporting concerns and alternate opinions without fear of reprisal, and not punishing mistakes while still holding individuals accountable for misconduct (§1 c(1)-(2), p.2).
- Employees and supervisors are authorized to suspend or refuse work presenting an unacceptable risk of death or serious physical harm (§5 i-j, p.5); Center Directors and Center SMA Directors hold the parallel authority to suspend any activity presenting unacceptable risk (§5 g(3), §5 h(4), p.5).

- The Administrator approves acceptable crew-safety risk levels and authorizes or consents to exposing the public, workforce, property, or environment to potential harm from NASA activities, with delegation possible to the Associate Administrator, Chief SMA, Chief Health and Medical Officer, or Center Directors (§5 a(1)-(2), p.3).
- Modal force of the whole six-page extracted text: 0 "shall," 1 "should," 3 "may" — the directive is entirely declarative policy (our count).

## Where it stops

- **Policy and accountability only.** It names no engineering method and levies no numbered requirement; implementation is delegated entirely to the NPR family beneath it. It reaches JPL only through contract (§2 a, p.2).
- States its own interpretive convention: "may" or "can" denote discretion, "should" denotes recommended-not-required practice, "will" denotes expected outcome, and "are/is" denotes descriptive material (§2 b, p.2) — so a sentence written with "is" states a fact or a scope, not a duty.

## AI and machine-learning touchpoints

- Searched the full 6-page extracted text, case-insensitive, for "artificial intelligence," "machine learning," "autonom*," "data-driven," "model," and even "software": **zero occurrences of every one of those terms.** NASA's top-level safety and mission-success policy never names software, let alone AI or ML.
- 🔑 **Two clauses make an AI assurance effort an application of existing policy rather than a new one (our reading):**

> "When there is no accepted standard to manage novel or unique hazards, consulting with subject matter experts on strategies to ensure an acceptable level of risk."

— NPD 8700.1F, §1 b(4), p.2

> "Flexibility is allowed in the selection and acceptance of derived crew safety and mission success objectives, associated strategies, standards and requirements if the associated risks are understood, documented, and consistent with the established risk posture."

— NPD 8700.1F, §1 a(2)(a), p.1

  The first describes a hazard with no accepted standard, answered by expert consultation — which is the situation an unfamiliar AI-driven hazard presents. The second is the policy basis for a Technical Authority accepting a different standard in place of a named one, which is the same lever NPR 8715.3E §1.2 delegates for mission-assurance relief (our reading; see `NPR_8715.3.md`).
- Clause §1 a(2)(e) — "substantiated, monitored, and independently evaluated... based on systematic argumentation, explicit assumptions, and objective evidence" — states the *form* an assurance argument must take and never names who or what produces the work it argues about. Nothing in that sentence weakens because a learned component sits inside the system; satisfying it gets harder, because stated assumptions and objective evidence are exactly what a learned component tends to supply poorly (our reading).

## Traps

- **Treating the absence of "shall" as the absence of duty.** NPDs assign duties in the form "is responsible and accountable for" rather than with modal verbs; a modal-only search over this document finds nothing while missing real, load-bearing assignments of responsibility.
- **Reading an "are/is" sentence as an obligation.** The document's own §2 b convention marks "are/is" as descriptive, not mandatory — the same convention NPR 8000.4C states for its own text (see `NPR_8000.4C.md`).
- **Treating §1 b(4) as evidence that NASA has no answer for AI-shaped hazards.** It is the opposite: NASA's stated answer for a hazard with no accepted standard is to consult subject matter experts, not to leave the hazard unaddressed.
- **Assuming the Technical Authority accepts or rejects a project's engineering decisions directly.** §5 d limits the Technical Authority's role to concurrence or non-concurrence on risk-acceptance decisions and on the adequacy of the assurance case — not direct engineering sign-off.

## Verified quotes

> "It is NASA policy to assure acceptable levels of flight crew safety and mission success risk by: (1) Establishing a risk posture for crew safety (spaceflight crew and aircrew) and mission success, considering the potential benefits and strategic importance of the mission(s) and consequences of failure, to inform decisions regarding the formulation, implementation, and assurance of the mission."
— NPD 8700.1F, §1 a(1), p.1

> "Progress towards the accomplishment of crew safety and mission success objectives is substantiated, monitored, and independently evaluated throughout the lifecycle based on systematic argumentation, explicit assumptions, and objective evidence."
— NPD 8700.1F, §1 a(2)(e), p.1

> "When there is no accepted standard to manage novel or unique hazards, consulting with subject matter experts on strategies to ensure an acceptable level of risk."
— NPD 8700.1F, §1 b(4), p.2

> "Concur or non-concur with risk acceptance decisions involving or significantly affecting risk to safety and mission success... Concur or non-concur with the adequacy of assurance cases for safety and mission success in support of relevant decision authorities."
— NPD 8700.1F, §5 d(1)-(2), p.4

> "Employees are authorized to refuse any work activity that would expose them to a risk of death or serious physical harm they deem unacceptable."
— NPD 8700.1F, §5 j, p.5
