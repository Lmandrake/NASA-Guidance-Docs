## What it is for, plainly
NASA's policy on personal use of Government-furnished IT equipment, services, and resources — what limited personal use is allowed, who is responsible for enforcing the limits, and what software or equipment a NASA IT user may not install, connect, or use on NASA-furnished systems.

## What it obliges
- §1.a: permits "limited acceptable personal use" of Government-furnished property when it does not overburden Agency IT resources, interfere with official business, or create more than minimal additional cost (p.1).
- §1: users "have no expectation of privacy" on NASA IT at any time, including during personal use (pp.1-2).
- §5 names five body paragraphs, each a colon-terminated "shall" stem binding one role:
  - §5.a: the Office of the Chief Information Officer "shall" implement and maintain this directive and ensure sufficient Agency-level controls for personal-use monitoring (p.3).
  - §5.b: Information System Owners (ISOs) "shall... Authorize limited installation of software necessary for mission functions with the documented approval of the system AO," and must ensure such software meets supply-chain requirements, is properly licensed, and comes from a safe, authorized source (pp.3-4).
  - §5.c: Contracting Officers/Agreement Managers "shall" ensure contractors are informed of, and comply with, the terms governing use of Government IT resources (p.4).
  - Supervisors "shall" permit allowable personal use and pursue sanctions for misuse, including disciplinary action (pp.4-5).
  - §5.f: NASA IT Users "shall not download, copy, or install unapproved or unauthorized software applications or data programs onto NASA IT," with a non-exhaustive example list — screen savers, games, "free, test, trial, or demo software," unauthorized remote-access and proxy-bypass services, virtual machines (p.5).
- Attachment G lists specific banned-company software/equipment (Huawei, ZTE, Hytera, Hikvision, Dahua, Kaspersky, China Mobile International USA, China Telecom Americas) that NASA IT users may not install or connect.
- Attachment A defines "Unapproved or Unauthorized Software or Services" as applications/services lacking a NASA Authorization to Operate (ATO) or otherwise not approved for use, including FedRAMP-authorized services that have not separately completed NASA's own Assessment and Authorization process.

## Where it stops
- The body text carries five "shall" paragraph-stems (OCIO, ISOs, Contracting Officers/Agreement Managers, Supervisors, NASA IT Users); NASA's own Attachment C Requirements Matrix indexes 70 numbered requirements against those same five roles, almost all without their own modal verb — force in Attachment C is inherited from the body stem, not restated (our reading; see ../force.md on stem-inherited force generally).
- A search of the full extracted body text for "artificial," "AI," and "generative" returns zero hits — this directive does not mention generative AI, chatbots, or AI tools anywhere in its own text, including in the unapproved-software examples (our own grep, this pass).
- Applies to JPL "only to the extent specified in the NASA/Caltech Prime Contract" (§2, p.2) — the NPD text alone does not reach JPL directly.

## AI and machine-learning touchpoints
- Nothing in this directive is AI-specific. Its only point of contact with an AI tool is structural: §5.b's requirement that an ISO "Authorize limited installation of software necessary for mission functions" before any software is installed on NASA IT would, on its own wording, reach an AI coding assistant, a local model, or an AI browser extension exactly as it reaches any other unapproved application — no different test, no AI carve-out (our reading).
- §5.f's "unapproved or unauthorized software applications" language and Attachment A's ATO-based definition would, by the same inheritance logic, require any AI service NASA IT users want to run (a hosted chatbot, a cloud-based coding tool) to go through the same Authorization to Operate / supply-chain-vetting path as any other software or service, rather than a route built for AI specifically (our reading).

## How it connects to other documents
- Supersedes NPD 2540.1I, "Personal Use of Government Office Equipment Including Information Technology," dated August 19, 2019 (reference list).
- Cites NPD 2810.1 (NASA Information Security Policy), NPR 1382.1 (NASA Privacy Procedural Requirements), NPR 2810.2 (Possession and Use of NASA Information and Information Systems Outside of the United States), and ITS-HBK-SCRM.2810.v1.0.0 (Information and Communications Technology Supply Chain Risk Management) among applicable documents.
- Attachment C requirements cross-reference the body's lettered "shall" paragraphs (e.g., "5a(2)" maps back to the OCIO paragraph, "f." to NASA IT Users) — our check found a wrong-parent mismatch in 29 of the 70 Attachment C rows (41%), a NASA self-consistency issue, not something resolved here.

## Traps
- Counting only the body's "shall" sentences and concluding there are 7 (or 5) requirements: NASA's own Attachment C publishes 70, almost all modal-free children inheriting force from the body's lettered stems. Count by Attachment C for a complete picture.
- Assuming "generative AI" or "AI tools" are addressed somewhere in this directive because it governs software installation generally: a direct text search finds zero occurrences of "artificial," "AI," or "generative" anywhere in the body.
- Treating the JPL/contractor applicability line as making this directive binding on JPL by itself: §2 ties it to "the NASA/Caltech Prime Contract" specifically, not to NASA directives generally.
