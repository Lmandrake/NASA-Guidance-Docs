## What it is for, plainly

This NPR sets the Agency's two companion risk-management processes and requires every organizational unit — Headquarters offices, Centers, programs, and projects — to run both of them. **Risk-Informed Decision Making (RIDM)** evaluates risk and alternatives before a decision is locked in; **Continuous Risk Management (CRM)** tracks and manages risk during execution, against the baseline those decisions set. A unit manager must ensure both processes run, write a Risk Management Plan, and treat risk acceptance and requirement waivers as formal, accountable acts.

## What it obliges (or advises)

- Every unit manager **shall** ensure the two processes run and that key decisions are risk-informed: "Ensure that the RIDM and CRM processes are implemented within the unit and that key decisions of the organizational unit are risk-informed." (§3.2.2 a, p.20)
- The manager **shall** write a Risk Management Plan stating the unit's scope, sources of risk, and the rigor it will apply (§3.2.2, p.20).
- The manager **shall** ensure risk identification is comprehensive: "Risk identification is comprehensive and consistent with the baseline performance requirements of that unit." (§3.4.2 a, p.25)
- Accepting a risk to safety or mission success is a formal, named, accountable act, and so is a requirement waiver that affects either: "All key project decisions that accept risks to safety or mission success — either implicitly or explicitly — are subject to the requirements of 3.5.3 on creation of the basis for the decision, TA or other cognizant authority... concurrence" (§3.5, p.27); the manager must trace each such decision to the unit's established risk posture and document it (§3.5.3, p.27).
- Effort scales to the stakes: "Both CRM and RIDM are applied within a graded approach" (§1.2.4.6, p.15); lower-cost, lower-risk-classification missions (e.g., CubeSats, Risk Class D) may instead follow the graded-approach directives of NPR 8705.4 (§3.2.3, p.24).
- When a decision cannot be made for lack of information, the disposition is **RESEARCH**: "When additional information is needed to make a decision, efforts to RESEARCH a risk (obtain additional information) are documented and tracked in the risk database." (§3.4.2 i(5), p.26) — one of five named dispositions alongside MITIGATE, WATCH, ACCEPT, and CLOSE.

- **Risk acceptance authority is Technical-Authority-concurred, not manager-alone.** A program/project risk-acceptance decision needs the basis for the decision, Technical Authority (or other cognizant authority) concurrence, and — where a human Risk Taker is involved — that person's manager's consent to the risk (§3.5, p.27). For institutional risk, the Center Director may formally delegate acceptance authority to a specific Center office manager, with a concurring authority analogous to the Technical Authority role, but the Center Director remains ultimately accountable (§3.6, p.29).

## Where it stops

- **Manages risk against objectives somebody wrote down; silent about shortfalls nobody stated as an objective.** This is the document's own definition, not our reading: "Risk. The potential for shortfalls with respect to achieving explicitly established and stated objectives." (Appendix A, p.34)
- A **Candidate Risk** is a defined term for exactly the pending, not-yet-adjudicated case: "A potential risk that has been identified and is pending adjudication by the affected programmatic or institutional authority." (Appendix A, p.32)

## AI and machine-learning touchpoints

- A case-insensitive text search of the public PDF for "artificial intelligence," "machine learning," and "autonom*": **zero occurrences.** This NPR itself does not name AI/ML anywhere.
- §P.1 c (p.3) commits the directive to reach "all sources of risk, whether of a random or an intentional and adversarial nature, that originate internally or externally to NASA" — a mechanism-neutral scope statement that reaches a learned component inside a system with no edit (our reading), even though the text never says so.
- §3.4.2 a's comprehensiveness duty is bounded by the Appendix A definition of Risk: a shortfall only counts if it falls short of an "explicitly established and stated objective." A learned component can fail in a way that maps to no stated performance requirement — in which case the shortfall is real but is not a "risk" under this NPR's own definition, and never enters the list to be managed (our reading). ⚠️ This is a limit shared with any large, heavily integrated conventional system, not something distinctive to AI/ML.
- 🔴 **NASA guidance beneath this NPR does name AI/ML — the NPR itself does not.** The companion *NASA Risk Management Handbook, Part 1* (NASA/SP-20240014019, Office of Safety and Mission Assurance), which supplies this NPR's methodology, states in §3.2.4 "Unknown and/or Underappreciated Risk," immediately after its Figure 3-5:

> "Novel and complex uses of artificial intelligence (AI), including machine learning (ML), can contribute to risks that are not easily captured by traditional analysis of known failure modes. Additionally, AI/ML models can be non-deterministic and highly complex resulting in a high potential for U/U risk. Additional information on AI risk management frameworks can be found in the NIST AI Risk Management Framework [20] and the NIST AI Risk Management Framework Playbook [21]."

— *NASA Risk Management Handbook, Part 1*, §3.2.4, p.73

  This single paragraph is the handbook's only occurrence of "artificial intelligence" or "machine learning" in 303 pages, and it creates no requirement — it classifies AI/ML-driven risk as a named category ("Unknown and/or Underappreciated," or U/U, risk) and defers outward to a NIST framework rather than stating NASA criteria of its own. **So "no NASA document names AI" is false for this handbook — it names AI/ML and defers to NIST.** The handbook is guidance supplementing NPR 8000.4C, where the "shall" statements live; whether any agency requirement is AI-specific is not established by this handbook.

## How it connects to other documents

- Cancels NPR 8000.4B, dated December 6, 2017 (§P.6, p.4).
- Its methodology is detailed in the *NASA Risk Management Handbook* — Part 1 (NASA/SP-20240014019, "Concepts and Principles," 303 pages, supersedes NASA/SP-2011-3422) and Part 2 (NASA/SP-20240014326, "Application Examples," 89 pages). Part 1's front matter states it "supersedes NASA SP-2011-3422 Version 1 dated November 2011."
- §1.2.4.6 (p.15) points to NPR 8705.4 (Risk Classification for NASA Payloads) for the graded approach on lower-cost, lower-risk-classification missions.
- The handbook's §4.7.3.9 (pp.134-136) names NASA-STD-7009 (Standard for Models and Simulations) as the source of a credibility assessment scale for any model or simulation used as a risk-analysis input — the mechanism by which a model used as an *analysis tool* in a NASA risk decision acquires a credibility obligation (our reading; NASA-STD-7009B is a separate document in this reference set).

- For "Authority roles regarding risk," the NPR defers outward rather than restating the material: "For additional information, see NPD 1000.0 with regard to the topic of 'Authority roles regarding risk.'" (§1.2, p.5) — so a complete reading of who may accept what risk needs NPD 1000.0 alongside this NPR.

## Traps

- **A document's own conventions section outranks its definitions appendix.** The Preface states: "'are/is' denotes descriptive material" (p.4). Appendix A's definition of Risk reads "Risk **is** operationally characterized..." — descriptive by the document's own rule, not an obligation, even though it sits in a numbered appendix.
- **Mistaking the handbook's AI/ML paragraph for an NPR requirement.** It is guidance in a companion SP-series handbook, one paragraph in 303 pages, with no "shall." Citing it as if NPR 8000.4C itself required something about AI/ML risk overstates its force.
- **Treating RESEARCH as a stall tactic.** It is a named, documented disposition under §3.4.2 i(5) for exactly the case where information is genuinely insufficient to decide — not an undocumented default.
- Searching only for "AI" or "ML" as whole words will miss the paragraph above if automated: the handbook's acronym table and figure caption are the only other places those strings appear, so a naive keyword count can undercount or overcount this finding depending on boundary handling.
