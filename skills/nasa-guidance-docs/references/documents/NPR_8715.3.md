## What it is for, plainly

Revision D was NASA's general safety-program rulebook: hazard control order, failure-tolerance and redundancy rules for safety-critical operations, inhibit counts, and system-safety roles. **Revision E is a different, much narrower document that reused the same NPR number.** Its own Preface states the change directly:

> "This directive has been significantly updated from the initial release of revision D. The procedural requirements for requesting relief from Agency mission assurance requirements, previously contained in Section 1.13, is the only content that remains."

— NPR 8715.3E, §P.1, p.3

Everything else in revision D was either cancelled outright or moved to a different document. Revision E's own Preface says where each piece went, in its own words:

> "This change cancels most requirements previously contained in chapter 1, Programmatic Safety Requirements. NPR 8705.2, Human-Rating Requirements for Space Systems, and NPR 8705.4, Risk Classification for NASA Payloads, provide requirements for mission success of crewed and robotic space flight missions respectively."

> "This change cancels procedural requirements previously contained in chapter 2, System Safety. Accepted standards and guidance for the conduct of system safety include NASA/SP-2010-580 ... NASA/SP-2014-612 ... MIL-STD-882 ... and ISO 14620-1 ... Technical Authorities may accept other standards for use by programs and projects."

— NPR 8715.3E, §P.1, p.3

So Chapter 1's programmatic safety text went to NPR 8705.2 and NPR 8705.4 (requirements documents), while Chapter 2's system-safety text went to **handbooks and standards a Technical Authority may substitute** — not to another "shall"-bearing NPR. Nuclear safety content went to NPR 8715.26, and institutional-safety content (public safety, operational safety, aviation safety, fire safety, safety training, facility safety) went to NPR 8715.1 (§P.1, p.3). What survived in NPR 8715.3 itself, under the new title, is the procedure for asking to be excused from an Agency mission-assurance requirement.

## What it obliges (or advises) — the live Revision E

- Compliance is the default, and asking for relief is a normal, expected act rather than an admission of failure: "It is Agency policy that all requirements are complied with unless relief is formally granted. Requesting relief from requirements is both an expected and accepted part of establishing proper requirements." (§1.1, p.5)
- The Chief, Safety and Mission Assurance (SMA) is the Approving Authority for relief from Agency mission assurance requirements, and delegates that authority — **including accepting an alternate technical standard in place of the named one** — to the program or project SMA Technical Authority, keeping six areas back for the Chief, SMA alone: orbital debris mitigation, planetary protection, nuclear flight safety, human-rating of spaceflight systems, mission risk classification, and mishap investigations under NPR 8621.1 (§1.2, pp.5-6).
- A relief request must state, in writing: the requirement, the nature of the departure, the reason, the resulting change in risk to the public/workforce/property/environment, the resulting change in risk to crew safety and mission success, confirmation of no conflict with higher-level requirements, whether and when compliance will be achieved, alternate risk-management actions, and the program/project SMA Technical Authority's own findings on the merits (§1.3, p.6).
- Relief does not take effect until approved **and** the added risk has been accepted by the appropriate authority; "NPR 8000.4 contains requirements for decisions to accept risk to safety and mission success." (§1.3, p.6)
- NASA cannot grant relief from an external (Federal, state, or local) requirement; those requests are forwarded to the issuing agency (§1.1, p.5).

## Where it stops

- **A procedure for departing from a requirement, not a requirement about how anything is built, verified, or made safe.** Revision E states no engineering content of its own.
- **Agency mission-assurance requirements only** — relief from an Agency *institutional safety* requirement goes through NPR 8715.1 instead (§P.1, p.3).
- The six retained areas (above) can only be delegated back by the Chief, SMA personally; a project Technical Authority cannot grant relief in them unilaterally (§1.2, pp.5-6).

## AI and machine-learning touchpoints

- A case-insensitive text search of both revisions for "artificial intelligence," "machine learning," and "autonom*": **zero hits in each.** Neither document names AI/ML anywhere.
- 🔴 **The "Safety Critical" definition-from-another-NPR trap.** NPR 7150.2D, Appendix A, p.49, defines "Safety Critical" and tags its source: "(Source NPR 8715.3)." That definition's text — "A term describing any condition, event, operation, process, equipment, or system that could cause or lead to severe injury, major damage, or mission failure if performed or built improperly, or allowed to remain uncorrected" — **does exist in cancelled Revision D's own glossary**, worded almost identically: "Safety Critical. Term describing any condition, event, operation, process, equipment, or system that could cause or lead to severe injury, major damage, or mission failure if performed or built improperly, or allowed to remain uncorrected." (8715.3D, Appendix A. Definitions, p.40). **It is absent from the live Revision E** — searched Revision E's full text for "critical": zero occurrences, and its only glossary sections are an Acronyms list and a References list (§Appendix A-B, p.7), with no Definitions appendix at all. So NPR 7150.2D's own source attribution now points at cancelled text; the definition survives only because 7150.2D copied it forward before 8715.3D was cancelled (our reading).
- The failure-tolerance clauses that used to carry this topic — "stop at the first stable configuration" and "the assumption of failure independence" — are **cancelled Revision D text, not live requirements.** See Traps below.

## How it connects to other documents

- NPR 8715.3E's own Authority is NPD 8700.1, NASA Policy for Safety and Mission Success (§P.3, p.4).
- NPR 8715.3E §1.3 (p.6) invokes NPR 8000.4 for the formal risk-acceptance act that must accompany any granted relief.
- NPR 8715.3E §1.2 (pp.5-6) names NPR 8715.1 (institutional safety), NPR 8705.2 (human-rating), NPR 8705.4 (risk classification), NPR 8715.26 (nuclear flight safety), and NPR 8621.1 (mishap investigation) as the documents governing the six retained areas.
- Cancelled Revision D named NPR 8621.1 for mishap-investigation policy generally ("Policies, requirements, and procedures for mishap investigations are provided in NPR 8621.1," §1.1, p.8) — not reviewed here.

## Traps

- **A page count is not a completeness measure.** Two renderings of Revision D exist at 54 and 47 pages; the 47-page rendering is the *later* state, because a cancelled appendix (Appendix D, radioactive-material tables) was removed from it. Its own Change Log carries two rows the 54-page rendering lacks. Use the change log, never the page count, to tell which is current.
- **Reading NPR 8715.3 as still containing failure-tolerance and inhibit rules.** The phrases "stable configuration" and "assumption of failure independence" each occur exactly once among the documents mapped here — both on cancelled Revision D pages. The nearest *live* text on the same topic is NASA/SP-2014-612 p.181, a review-step in a System Safety Handbook a Technical Authority **may replace with another standard** — guidance a project can select away from, not an Agency "shall."
- **NODIS page-footer offset is specific to this 47-page Revision-D rendering.** Its footers sit at the foot of the page they close, so page citations above for Revision D follow the printed page footers.
- **Confusing a reused NPR number for continuity of content.** Revision E is not an update of Revision D's safety-program material; it is a near-total replacement that kept only the relief procedure and renamed the whole document around it.
