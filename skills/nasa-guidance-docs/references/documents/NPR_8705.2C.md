## What it is for, plainly

This NPR defines the process by which NASA certifies a crewed space system as fit to carry people — the Human-Rating Certification (HRC). A crewed system must obtain the certification before its first crewed flight and keep it for the system's whole life (§P.1.e, p.4). Change 2 moved the technical content (the actual engineering requirements a human-rated system must meet) out of this NPR and into a separate standard, NASA-STD-8719.29; this document now carries the certification *process*, not the technical requirements themselves.

## What it obliges (or advises)

- A crewed space system **shall** hold a Human-Rating Certification before its first crewed flight and **shall** maintain it for the system's life (§P.1.e, p.4).
- The Program Manager **shall** develop and maintain a Human-Rating Certification Package (HRCP) documenting the certification requirements (§2.2.1, p.22).
- The Program Manager **shall** document, in the HRCP, a safety analysis process to identify, evaluate, model and mitigate hazards and risks to crew safety "for the life of the program" (§2.2.3, p.23).
- The Program Manager **shall** conduct human-in-the-loop usability evaluation for human-system interfaces, with human performance criteria, for critical system and subsystem operations (§2.3.10.1, p.31).
- The Program Manager **shall** conduct a human error analysis for all mission phases, including response to system failures, and use it to understand catastrophic hazards human error could cause, understand risk and uncertainty, and influence design decisions (§2.3.11, pp.31–32).
- The Program Manager **shall** design the system to manage human error by a fixed precedence: first prevent the error, then reduce its likelihood and let the human detect/correct/recover from it, then limit the error's negative effects (§2.3.12, p.32).
- At Critical Design Review, the Program Manager **shall** document how testing will verify and validate "the performance, security, and safety of all critical software across the entire performance envelope," including off-nominal, contingency and fault-injected stress testing (§2.4.5.1–2, p.34).
- Certification is granted to the whole crewed space system, against named reference missions, with multiple Technical Authorities (Engineering, Safety and Mission Assurance, Health and Medical) and the Director, Johnson Space Center, concurring at five program milestones (§1.3.2, p.12; Appendix A definitions).
- Compliance is checked at the standing milestone reviews (SRR, SDR, PDR, CDR, SIR, ORR) and is "subject to audit and assessment in accordance with the requirements contained within NPR 8705.6, Safety and Mission Assurance Audits, Reviews, and Assessments" (§P.5, p.7).
- Requests for waivers, deviations and exceptions are dispositioned by the Chief, Safety and Mission Assurance, "subject to concurrence from the Engineering and Health and Medical Technical Authorities and the Director, JSC" where the request crosses Technical Authority boundaries or affects crew risk (§1.4.4.e, p.14).

## Where it stops

- **Carries process only; technical requirements moved to NASA-STD-8719.29.** Since Change 2, Chapter 3's engineering requirements (failure tolerance, inadvertent-action protection, and the rest) live in that standard, which is not reviewed here. Any statement about whether a specific technical requirement reaches a learned component is a statement about NASA-STD-8719.29, not about this NPR.
- **Scoped to crewed systems.** It does not grade or certify uncrewed robotic payloads; that is NPR 8705.4's business (see `NPR_8705.4B.md`). The two documents divide the work: 8705.4 grades robotic payload risk tolerance, 8705.2C certifies crewed systems, and neither labels the other's subject (§P.2.b, p.4; our reading).
- **Reference-mission-bounded.** Certification applies to "the certified envelope of the reference mission(s)" (§2.6.5.b, p.37); operating outside that envelope is outside the certification as written.
- The Rationale and Note blocks that accompany most numbered requirements are the document's own explanatory material, not independently binding text — they explain the "shall" beside them but create no separate obligation (our reading, consistent with the modal-verb convention stated at §P.2.j, p.6: "a requirement is identified by 'shall,' descriptive material by 'is,' and permission by 'may'").

## AI and machine-learning touchpoints

- The document contains no AI- or ML-specific clause; "autonom*" occurs only in the Definitions appendix, whose "Automated" and "Autonomous" entries describe control relative to a human or Earth-based system, not a learned model (Appendix A, p.39; our reading).
- §2.3.11's human error analysis and §2.3.12's error-management precedence are written about *human* error specifically; nothing in the text extends either requirement to a machine-generated decision inside the human-in-the-loop chain (our reading) — a question this NPR's text does not reach.
- §2.3.7.1.d's note on evaluating crew-survival capability names "autonomy" among the properties an evaluation should weigh for human-interaction design ("functionality, performance, reliability, availability, autonomy, response, activation features, and whether the design requires human interaction," p.28) — autonomy here means the capability's degree of automatic operation, not a reference to an AI/ML component (our reading).
- §2.4.5's critical-software test requirement (fault-injected, off-nominal, full performance envelope) is written as a testing obligation over software behavior generally; it does not distinguish a conventionally coded component from a learned one, so it would apply to either as written (our reading).
- Its own framing of human-rating as assuring a capability — "Human-rating is the process of designing, evaluating, and assuring that the total system can safely conduct the required human missions" (§1.2.2.b(1), p.10) — judges what the system does rather than how a part of it was built, which is a shape that does not inherently break if a learned component sits inside the certified system (our reading).

## How it connects to other documents

- Carries its certification requirements forward from the technical requirements in NASA-STD-8719.29, which this NPR references but does not itself contain (§P.4.c, p.6; Change History, p.3).
- Cites NPR 7120.5 for program/project management and milestone definitions (§P.4.a, p.6) and NPR 7120.5/7123.1 for the milestone reviews used to verify compliance (§P.5, p.7).
- Cites NPR 8715.3 for the exception/deviation/waiver process and for hazard-analysis guidance (§2.2.2 Note, p.23; §2.3.2 Note, p.26).
- Cites NASA-STD-3001 Volumes 1 and 2 (crew health; human factors, habitability and environmental health) as applicable standards (§2.2.5, p.24).
- Cites NPR 8705.6 (Safety and Mission Assurance Audits, Reviews, and Assessments) as the audit mechanism over this NPR's own process and requirements (§P.5, p.7).
- Does not itself reference NPR 8705.4; the two documents' mutual carve-out (crewed systems vs. robotic payloads) is read across both documents' applicability clauses, not stated by either pointing to the other (our reading; see `NPR_8705.4B.md` §P.2.c).

## Traps

- Quoting "the human-rating requirements" as a single document: since Change 2, that phrase spans two documents — this NPR (process) and NASA-STD-8719.29 (technical requirements, not reviewed here).
- Treating a Note or Rationale block as a separate requirement: the document's convention makes only "shall" sentences binding; Notes and Rationale explain intent and are advisory/explanatory (§P.2.j, p.6; our reading).
- Assuming "autonomous" or "automated" in this NPR means an AI/ML system: both terms are defined relative to Earth-based or human control, not to a learned model (Appendix A, p.39).
- Assuming the long gap between the 2017 effective date and 2028 expiration date reflects an unusually long NPR term: it results from Change 2's 2024 edit, not from the original 2017 issuance.

> "The human-rating requirements in this NPR apply to the development and operation of crewed space systems developed by NASA and used to conduct NASA human spaceflight missions. This NPR may apply to other crewed space systems when documented in separate requirements or agreements."
— NPR 8705.2C, §P.2.b, p.4

> "This NPR requires applicable space systems as defined in paragraph P.2 to obtain a Human-Rating Certification prior to the first crewed mission and maintain the rating throughout the systems' life cycle."
— NPR 8705.2C, §P.1.e, p.4

> "Human-rating is the process of designing, evaluating, and assuring that the total system can safely conduct the required human missions."
— NPR 8705.2C, §1.2.2.b(1), p.10

> "The Program Manager shall conduct a human error analysis for all mission phases to include operations planned for response to system failures."
— NPR 8705.2C, §2.3.11.1, p.31

> "At CDR, the Program Manager shall document, as part of the HRCP, how testing will be used to verify and validate the performance, security, and safety of all critical software across the entire performance envelope (or flight envelope) including mission functions, modes, and transitions (with rationale)."
— NPR 8705.2C, §2.4.5.1, p.34

> "The release of the NASA-STD-8719.29, NASA Technical Requirements for Human-Rating, was a direct transfer of the technical requirements from NPR 8705.2C, Change 1 chapter 3, and did not introduce any new requirements or changes to existing requirements."
— NPR 8705.2C, Change History, Chg# 2, p.3

> "Note 3: Human-Rating Certifications, per this NPR, are based on reference missions. During the reference missions, the crewed space system interfaces with other systems (control centers, launch pads, space communication systems). Some of the requirements in NASA-STD-8719.29... such as failure tolerance and inadvertent action requirements, cross the interface to other systems."
— NPR 8705.2C, §P.2.b Note 3, p.5

> "The Program Manager shall design the system to manage human error according to the following precedence: a. Design the system to prevent human error in the operation and control of the system. b. Design the system to reduce the likelihood of human error and provide the capability for the human to detect and correct or recover from the error. c. Design the system to limit the negative effects of errors."
— NPR 8705.2C, §2.3.12, p.32
